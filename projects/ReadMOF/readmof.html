Theme 2: Chemical Representations of Materials

ReadMOF Project Overview

Structure-free semantic embeddings from systematic MOF nomenclature.

ReadMOF treats systematic MOF names as structured chemical language. The project asks whether pretrained language models can extract metal, ligand, oxidation-state, bridging, and coordination information from names alone, then use that representation for property prediction, electronic screening, and chemical reasoning.

ReadMOF Project Overview

Each panel below introduces one stage of the complete ReadMOF story. The main narrative and technical notes are merged by topic, so the representation boundary, model comparisons, ablations, screening details, and implementation notes appear where they are used in the argument.

01 MOF structural data are noisy and structure-based models are costly. Motivation and problem setting

MOFs are crystalline porous materials assembled from metal nodes and multidentate organic linkers, with applications in gas storage, separation, CO2 capture, catalysis, sensing, thermal management, and drug delivery.[1-10] Their modular chemistry makes them attractive for data-driven discovery.[11-17] Most computational workflows, however, still rely on atomic coordinates, CIF parsing, molecular graphs, or geometry-derived descriptors.

The limitation is data fidelity. Computation-ready MOF databases can contain chemically consequential inconsistencies, including misassigned oxidation states, missing hydrogens, missing or unresolved solvent molecules, structural disorder, and ambiguous unit cells.[18-35] Since graph and coordinate descriptors are sensitive to these details, structural noise can affect property prediction, candidate ranking, and reproducibility. ReadMOF starts from this pressure point: a useful representation should remain chemically expressive while reducing dependence on perfect atomistic structure.

Representation boundary

A useful boundary is that systematic nomenclature is chemically structured but geometrically abstracted.[36-37] It does not encode exact coordinates, bond lengths, bond angles, flexibility, or long-range topology. The claim is therefore not that names replace atomistic structure, but that names preserve composition, coordination environment, metal nuclearity, ligand identity, and functional-group signals that are often enough for the property classes studied here.

The complementary design principle is that geometric descriptors can be combined with name embeddings in the same pipeline when a task depends on precise geometry. ReadMOF is positioned as a scalable representation for noisy, incomplete, or early-stage structural data, not as a universal substitute for high-quality CIF-based modeling.

Technical takeaway: Establish why a structure-free or structure-light representation is valuable, especially for early-stage database exploration and high-throughput screening.

02 Systematic names are chemical abstractions of MOF structure. Chemical abstraction

Systematic MOF names are not arbitrary labels. IUPAC-style nomenclature encodes metal identity, oxidation state, metal nuclearity, ligand identity, ligand multiplicity, bridging mode, coordination role, functional groups, and polymeric extension.[38] These names do not specify exact atomic coordinates, bond lengths, bond angles, framework flexibility, or detailed long-range topology, but they do provide a standardized chemical abstraction of building units and coordination environments.

Using IRMOF-1 as the entry example, the name catena-(tris(μ4-terephthalato)-(μ4-oxo)-tetra-zinc) identifies a Zn4O node, a central μ4-oxo bridge, three μ4-terephthalato linkers, and periodic extension into a framework.[39] This is the key boundary: systematic names are chemically structured but geometrically abstracted.

Technical takeaway: Convert the name from a database field into a chemically meaningful representation target.

03 ReadMOF converts systematic names into language-model embeddings. Embedding workflow

ReadMOF tokenizes each systematic MOF name and passes it through a pretrained text encoder to obtain a fixed-length dense embedding. This builds on broader work in text-derived materials representations, dense retrieval, multitask text encoders, molecular language models, MOF identification schemes, and structure-aware MOF machine learning.[40-60] The embedding is intended to capture the chemical entities and coordination descriptors embedded in the name, without constructing atomic structures, parsing CIF files, or generating molecular graphs.

The implementation evaluates pretrained encoders from SentenceTransformers and Hugging Face, including SBERT, BGE, E5, Nomic AI, ChemicalBERT, MatSciBERT, SciBERT, and BERT families.[61-82] Names are embedded with normalized output vectors and task-specific prefixes such as clustering, classification, or search_query. These embeddings are then used for similarity analysis, retrieval, clustering, structural-property prediction, electronic-property prediction, screening, and LLM reasoning.

Dataset and embedding implementation

The implementation starts from systematic names and metadata obtained from the CSD February 2025 release. Filtering retained polymeric MOFs with valid systematic names, formulae, crystal systems, organic-content indicators, and non-ambiguous solvent information, yielding 31,103 MOFs for similarity, clustering, and retrieval. Electronic prediction used 10,716 entries aligned with QMOF, while generative reasoning used 3,176 RetChemQA question-answer pairs from 96 matched publications. The supporting software stack includes common materials, embedding, optimization, fine-tuning, interpretation, and data-analysis tools.[83-95]

The embedding vectors are unit-normalized and stored as compressed arrays for reuse. This matters for the project page because the representation is a reusable project asset, not only a one-off figure pipeline.

Technical takeaway: Define the central machine-learning object of the project: a structure-free, name-derived vector representation.

04 Name embeddings align with RAC structural descriptors and cluster by chemistry. Similarity and clustering

The first validation question is whether embeddings derived only from names preserve relationships that resemble structure-derived chemistry. ReadMOF compares name-embedding cosine similarity matrices with RAC similarity matrices across a filtered CSD set of 31,103 polymeric MOFs with validated systematic names.[20, 96-99] RAC descriptors are computed from structure graphs and atom-centered autocorrelations, providing a coordinate-aware baseline for local chemical environments.

The strongest text encoders, including Nomic embedding, BGE, and E5 variants, produce similarity structures closely aligned with RACs. The representative Nomic embedding model reaches a semantic-structural cosine similarity of 0.96. Two-dimensional t-SNE projections further show chemically coherent organization, with clusters dominated by common transition-metal centers such as Cu, Co, Ni, and Zn.

RAC baseline and encoder comparison

The RAC baseline is defined in detail: CIF structures are parsed with pymatgen and repaired with ASE when needed, bond graphs are built with CrystalNN, and atom groups are separated into metal centers, full linker atoms, linker connectors, and functional atoms. The non-redundant RAC vector contains 156 physically meaningful features from atomic properties, topological path lengths, and summary statistics.

The extended encoder analysis compares 21 language models. Nomic-embed-v1.5, BGE-large-en-v1.5, and E5-large show clear block-like cosine similarity and well-separated metal-centered t-SNE clusters, while smaller or less chemically resolved models give flatter similarity maps and more overlapping clusters. The interpretation is that the best name embeddings encode both discrete category separation and continuous chemical semantic distance.

Technical takeaway: Demonstrate that the name-derived embedding space is chemically organized before using it for prediction or screening.

05 Chemical organization is interpretable through coordination roles and motifs. Coordination motifs

ReadMOF interprets the embedding organization by examining the chemical information explicitly carried by systematic names. Bridging annotations such as μ, μ2, μ4, μ6, and μ8 describe ligand coordination roles, while metal nuclearity and ligand identity define chemically constrained secondary building units. The project uses examples including IRMOF-1, MOF-274, UiO-66, MIL-88B, ZIF-8, HKUST-1, and DUT-10 to show that similar ligands can still imply different framework motifs when metal nodes and coordination roles change.[39, 100-105]

Higher coordination multiplicities tend to form more compact regions in embedding space, while lower-coordination descriptors are more dispersed. This does not mean that the model reconstructs full topology. It means that the systematic names provide chemically constrained signals that are reflected in the learned representation.

Coordination-role interpretation

The coordination labels are not decorative tokens. They isolate coordination multiplicity while preserving the ligand identity, so they help distinguish whether the same organic fragment is acting as a local linker, a high-connectivity bridge, or part of a denser secondary building unit. This is why motif-level comparisons are chemically meaningful even though the embedding never receives explicit 3D coordinates.

Technical takeaway: Explain why name embeddings can encode more than composition: they also carry coordination-role and motif-level information.

06 Name embeddings retrieve chemically related MOF analogues. Semantic retrieval

ReadMOF then tests whether name embeddings can retrieve related MOFs when the query is only a systematic name. In the representative example, a cobalt terephthalate target retrieves structures that share metal-ligand roles, ligand classes, or chemically analogous substitutions. RAC-based retrieval emphasizes strict geometric similarity, while name-based retrieval can connect structures across metal substitution, ligand isomerism, and naming variants such as terephthalato versus 1,4-benzenedicarboxylato.[106-110]

The retrieval summary supports the example by comparing chemical names, space groups, formulas, cell volumes, and densities for the target and retrieved structures. The retrieved frameworks remain close in overall structural properties, indicating that semantic similarity in systematic names can map to useful chemical analogy.

Retrieval evaluation

Retrieval is evaluated with Recall@100, Mean Reciprocal Rank, and NDCG, using RAC-based structural similarity as the relevance baseline.[111-114] The best encoders are also the ones with high RAC-alignment scores, supporting the link between semantic name structure and structural relevance.

07 Name embeddings predict structural and electronic properties. Property prediction

The project next asks whether name embeddings can support quantitative prediction. For structural properties, residual neural-network regressors trained on name embeddings predict largest cavity diameter, accessible surface area, density, and void fraction for a CSD-derived MOF subset. The reported R2 values exceed 0.88, and the distribution plots compare predicted and reference values for representative linker classes including BDC, BTC, and 4,4'-bipyridyl.

For electronic properties, ReadMOF aligns CSD entries with QMOF and predicts DFT-computed bandgaps under PBE, HLE17, HSE06-10HF, and HSE06 using 10,716 matched entries.[21-22, 97-98, 115-120] The models achieve R2 values above 0.90 across the reported functionals. The bandgap plots highlight metal-dependent trends: open-shell transition-metal cations with partially filled d shells, such as Cu2+, Ni2+, and Fe2+, tend to produce broader low-bandgap distributions, whereas closed-shell cations such as Zn2+, Cd2+, and Mg2+ produce narrower, higher-bandgap distributions.

The ablation studies explain the source of performance. Structural properties are most sensitive to ligand-related terms, while electronic bandgaps are most sensitive to metal identity and oxidation state. Masking metal centers causes the strongest degradation in bandgap prediction, supporting the chemical grounding of the learned representation.

Ablation and interpretability

The ablation study tests five controlled perturbations: masking structural modifiers such as catena, bis, and tris; masking only μx prefixes; masking full μx-ligand groups; masking all metal centers and oxidation states; and introducing spelling perturbations. These experiments separate lexical noise from chemically meaningful information loss.

For structural properties, the CSD MOF subset contains 54,556 entries. The structural-property ablation shows that removing ligand-related information causes the largest structural-property degradation, especially for ASA and density, while modifier masking has smaller effects. The SHAP analysis then gives a chemically consistent explanation: linker tokens dominate pore-size predictions, metal and connectivity tokens influence density, and modifier tokens affect void fraction by occupying or shaping pore volume.

For electronic properties, the QMOF-aligned set contains 10,716 entries. The electronic-property ablation shows that masking metal identity and oxidation state causes the strongest bandgap performance collapse, including drops to approximately 0.421 for PBE and 0.350 for HSE06. Token-level SHAP further identifies transition metals with partially filled d shells and conjugated N-donor ligands as strong negative contributors to bandgap, while closed-shell centers such as Mg2+ and Ca2+ become positive or neutral contributors under hybrid-functionals.

08 Bandgap prediction enables large-scale CSD screening for electronically active MOFs. Screening and retrospective validation

The trained name-based bandgap models are deployed as a front-end screening tool for 105,328 previously unseen CSD MOFs, with QMOF-overlapping entries excluded. Since electrical conductivity in MOFs is often associated with narrow bandgaps, predicted HSE06 bandgap is used to rank candidates for electronic activity.[124] The screening plot visualizes the predicted HSE06-PBE bandgap landscape, highlighting known conductive or semiconductive structures among the lowest predicted bandgap candidates.

Among the top-ranked candidates, 18 structures have previously reported semiconductive or conductive behavior. This provides retrospective enrichment evidence: systematic names alone can recover many electronically active frameworks from a large CSD search space.

09 Screening proposes new low-bandgap candidate MOFs. Prospective candidates

After rediscovering known electronically active materials, ReadMOF uses the same ranking to identify candidates whose conductive or semiconductive behavior has not yet been reported. These structures are not claimed as experimentally confirmed conductors; they are low-bandgap candidates prioritized for future experimental characterization or high-level computation.

10 Systematic names improve LLM chemical reasoning and interpretability. LLM reasoning

ReadMOF closes by testing whether systematic nomenclature is useful not only as an embedding input, but also as text for generative chemical reasoning. The project constructs two RetChemQA-derived datasets: one using shorthand framework identifiers such as MOF-14, and one replacing those identifiers with corresponding IUPAC-style systematic names.[187] Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct is fine-tuned under matched settings to isolate the effect of the naming representation.[188]

The reasoning examples cover formula-inference and synthesis-related questions. With shorthand identifiers, the model receives little explicit chemical content. With systematic names, terms such as tri-copper(ii), tribenzoato, and triaqua provide stoichiometric, ligand, and coordination cues. SHAP attribution becomes more localized on chemically meaningful input units, supporting both better factual grounding and more interpretable reasoning.

The quantitative evaluation reports the same trend after fine-tuning: the systematic-name dataset improves true/false accuracy and LLM-judge factual correctness relative to the shorthand-identifier setting. The mixed-metal and mixed-linker case study also clarifies the boundary of the approach: the model can parse metal and ligand entities, but may still compress polymeric catena structures into finite coordination units.

Reasoning evaluation

The reasoning evaluation uses a controlled protocol. Entities with MOF synonyms were matched between the CCDC MOF subset and RetChemQA by DOI, giving 96 publications and 3,176 QA pairs, including 975 true/false questions. A parallel dataset was built by replacing shorthand identifiers with systematic IUPAC-style names, while keeping the question-answer content and train/validation/test split controlled at the article level.

Open-ended answers were scored by a deterministic GPT-4o judge that evaluates chemical factual correctness from 0 to 1 while ignoring writing style.[189-191] True/false questions were evaluated by accuracy. The key result is not that raw pretrained models immediately benefit from names; the benefit appears after fine-tuning, where the systematic-name dataset supplies a learnable chemical signal.
